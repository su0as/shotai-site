Golf Ball Flight Laws Explained Simply

Every shot your ball takes tells you something specific about what happened at impact. Once you understand ball flight laws, you stop guessing and start diagnosing. That changes how you practice — and how fast you improve.

The two things that control ball flight

Every golf shot is shaped by two things at impact: the direction the clubface is pointing, and the direction the clubhead is travelling. That is it. Everything else — grip, stance, swing plane, tempo — only matters because it affects those two things.

  • Face angle controls where the ball starts. The ball launches roughly in the direction the face is pointing at impact.
  • Swing path relative to the face controls how the ball curves. If the path goes left of where the face points, the ball curves right (for a right-handed player). If the path goes right of where the face points, the ball curves left.

This replaced an older model that many golfers were taught — the idea that the ball starts along the path and curves toward the face. Modern launch monitors showed that the face accounts for roughly 75–85% of the initial start direction. The path relative to the face produces the spin that causes the curve.

The common shot shapes and what they mean

Straight

Face and path are pointing in the same direction at impact. The ball launches where you aimed and stays there. This is the goal, but it is also the rarest outcome because it requires both variables to be aligned simultaneously.

Fade (left-to-right for right-handers)

The ball starts to the left of target and curves right. Face is open relative to the path — or in plain terms, the path goes left of where the face points. A controlled fade is intentional. An uncontrolled one becomes a slice.

Draw (right-to-left for right-handers)

The ball starts to the right of target and curves left. Face is closed relative to the path — the path goes right of where the face points. A controlled draw is intentional. An uncontrolled one becomes a hook.

Push

The ball starts right and does not curve significantly. Face and path are aligned, but both are pointing right of target. Often a setup or alignment issue rather than an impact problem.

Pull

The ball starts left and does not curve significantly. Face and path are aligned, both pointing left of target. Also often a setup or alignment issue.

Slice

The ball starts roughly at or left of target and curves hard right. An extreme version of a fade. The face is significantly open relative to the path. The most common problem for newer golfers — usually caused by an out-to-in path combined with an open face at impact.

Hook

The ball starts roughly at or right of target and curves hard left. An extreme draw. The face is significantly closed relative to the path.

Why this changes how you practice

Before understanding ball flight laws, most golfers try to fix ball flight by changing random things — grip, stance, tempo — without knowing which variable is actually off. That produces inconsistent results because you might be changing the right thing, or you might not be.

With ball flight laws, you can work backwards. If your ball starts left and curves further left, you know your path is going left and your face is even further left relative to the path. Fix the face first, because the face controls start direction and accounts for most of the problem. Once start direction improves, then look at path to reduce the curve.

That sequence — face first, then path — is usually more efficient than trying to fix both at once.

How to use this at the range

Pick one shot shape and understand what it is telling you. If you are hitting a consistent push-fade (ball starts right, curves further right), your path is going out-to-in and your face is even further open. That points you toward two specific things to work on, not a vague "swing better" instruction.

Track start direction and curve direction separately. Ask: where did the ball start? Which way did it curve? Two answers. Two variables. One to fix at a time.
