Beginner Pickleball Practice Plan: First 30 Days

The first month of pickleball sets habits that either accelerate your improvement or slow it down for years. Players who spend week one randomly hitting and immediately playing games tend to build the same three or four errors into their game that they will later have to unlearn. This plan gives you a structured alternative — one that builds mechanics first, adds complexity gradually, and gives you a measurable benchmark at the end of each week.

Before you start: what you actually need

You do not need a perfect paddle, a court membership, or a regular partner to follow this plan. You need a paddle, a few balls, access to a wall or smooth surface for solo drilling, and ideally access to a court for two to three sessions per week. If you only have one session per week, the plan still works — it just takes longer to complete.

Set up your phone on a simple tripod or lean it against a bag for at least one session per week. You do not need to film everything. But reviewing your swing once a week closes the gap between what you think you are doing and what is actually happening.

Week 1: grip, ready position, and the dink

The first week is entirely about fundamentals. Do not play games yet if you can help it. The point is to build a baseline you can add to, not to start racking up wins and losses with mechanics you will need to change.

Session 1 — Grip and ready position: Spend 20 minutes working on continental grip and athletic stance. Shadow swing slowly. Get the feel of where the paddle face is at contact and where the follow-through ends. Film 10 shadow forehands and 10 shadow backhands. Watch them back.

Session 2 — Dinking at the wall: Stand at kitchen distance from a wall. Mark the net height (34 inches) with tape. Spend 15 minutes dinking softly to keep the ball below the mark. Count consecutive dinks. Your goal by the end of the week is 10 in a row.

Session 3 — Full session on a court: If you have a partner, spend the first half dinking cross-court from the kitchen. Spend the second half rallying from the baseline. Keep the swing compact and do not worry about winners. Focus only on your ready position reset after each shot.

Week 1 benchmark: Can you dink 10 consecutive shots keeping the ball below net height on the wall? Can you maintain an athletic ready position between shots without prompting?

Week 2: groundstrokes and serve mechanics

Week 2 adds the two shots that start every point: the serve and the return. Both are technically simpler than a dink but get practiced far less by beginners.

Session 1 — Serve practice: Set a target in the back third of the service box. Serve 30 balls, tracking how many hit your target zone. Then move the target to the opposite corner and repeat. You are training accuracy, not pace. Keep the serve consistent and deep rather than hard and short.

Session 2 — Groundstroke mechanics: Drop-and-hit forehand and backhand groundstrokes. Focus on short backswing, contact in front of the hip, and a consistent follow-through aimed at the target. Film a block of 10 forehands and watch the contact point. Is it in front of your body or beside it?

Session 3 — Combined drill on court: Serve, return, and move to the kitchen. The third shot drill: serve, let the partner return, and practice a third shot drop or drive into the kitchen zone. This is one of the hardest sequences in pickleball — getting the reps in early builds muscle memory that becomes automatic later.

Week 2 benchmark: Are 60% or more of your serves landing in the target zone? Can you hit 15 consecutive drop-and-hit groundstrokes to a target area without shanking?

Week 3: volleys and kitchen line play

Week 3 focuses on the shots you will use most during an actual rally — volleys at the kitchen and resets when you get driven back. This week requires a partner for most drills.

Session 1 — Volley mechanics: Stand at the kitchen line. Have your partner feed soft balls to your forehand and backhand alternately. Your goal: punch the ball with a short stroke, keeping the paddle face square and the contact in front of your body. No full swing. This is a compact, guided shot.

Session 2 — Dink rally: Cross-court dink rallies with a partner, both at the kitchen. Count how long you can sustain the rally without either player hitting the ball too high or out of the kitchen. 15 consecutive dinks is a solid beginner benchmark. 30 starts to feel like you understand the shot.

Session 3 — Play a game, track your misses: Play to 11 twice. After each game, write down the three most common types of errors: into the net, long, or popped up to be attacked. This is your feedback data for the next session's focus.

Week 3 benchmark: Can you sustain a 15-dink cross-court rally? Do you know what your most common error type is?

Week 4: putting it together and measuring progress

Week 4 is about connecting everything into a game context. You should be playing real points by now — with the mechanics you built over the first three weeks.

Session 1 — Video review session: Film a full rally practice. Review the recording for the three things you focused on during the month: grip, ready position, and dink face angle. How have they changed from week one? What is still showing up as a problem?

Session 2 — Match play with one focus: Play games, but carry a single technical cue from your week one focus. Every time you get to the kitchen, reset your ready position. Every dink you hit, check where the paddle face is. You are testing whether the mechanical change has transferred to real play under pressure.

Session 3 — Benchmark re-test: Return to the wall dink drill from week one. How many consecutive dinks can you hit below the net height mark now? Most players go from 10 to 25–35 in their first month of deliberate practice. That is a measurable, concrete improvement you can point to.

Month-end benchmark: Wall dink count, serve accuracy percentage, and a video comparison of your forehand from week one versus week four. These three data points tell you whether the month of practice produced a technical change or just more reps of the same thing.

How to continue after the first 30 days

After the first month, the structure stays the same but the complexity increases. Add the third shot drop as a deliberate drill. Start working on overhead smash mechanics. Introduce erne attempts and cross-court angles if you are playing with partners at a similar level. The principle stays constant: one technical focus per session, one video review per week, and match play to test whether the changes are actually transferring.
