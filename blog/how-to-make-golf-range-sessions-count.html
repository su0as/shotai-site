How to Make Golf Range Sessions Actually Count

Most golfers leave the range no better than when they arrived. A bucket of balls without a structure is not practice — it is just hitting. Here is how to build sessions that actually transfer to the course.

Shot AIRecord a few swings each session. What you feel and what you are doing are almost never the same thing.

The problem with mindless ball-hitting

When golfers say they practice a lot but are not improving, the problem is usually the same: they are reinforcing what they already do instead of building something new. Hitting 150 balls while thinking about the result — whether it went left, right, or straight — does not teach you how to change a movement. It just tells you the movement is consistent.

Quantity of practice matters far less than the quality of attention during it. One focused session of 40 balls beats a distracted bucket of 150.

Three practice formats that actually work

1. Block practice — for learning a new movement

Block practice means repeating the same shot, with the same club, while focusing on one specific change. You are trying to build a new motor pattern, so conditions need to be stable. Same target, same setup, same focus, over and over until the movement feels less foreign. This is where you take what a coach told you and try to make it real.

Block practice works best in the early stages of a change. It is not how you prepare for a round. It is how you engrain something new.

2. Variable practice — for transferring skills to the course

Once a movement is more consistent, variable practice helps it survive real conditions. Change clubs every shot. Change targets. Imagine different lies. Hit a draw, then a fade. Play imaginary holes from tee to green with a full pre-shot routine on every shot.

Variable practice is harder and results look messier, but it is what makes a skill usable when the pressure is on and the situation changes every hole.

3. Simulation practice — for building course confidence

Pick a hole you play regularly and simulate every shot from tee to green. Go through your full routine. Commit to a target. Accept the result like you would on the course. If the imaginary ball goes into a trap, play the next shot from that situation. This trains decision-making, not just movement.

The one-focus rule

Every session should have one technical priority. Not six. Not three. One. Before you hit a ball, know what you are working on. After the session, evaluate whether you made progress on that one thing — not whether you hit it well or poorly in general.

When players work on too many things at once, they work on nothing. The brain cannot process multiple mechanical changes simultaneously. Pick the most important one your coach or your video told you to fix, and stay on it.

Pick a target every single shot

This sounds obvious. Almost nobody does it. At the range, it is easy to aim vaguely at a general direction and hit. On the course, every shot has a specific target. The gap between those two habits is part of why range work does not transfer.

Before every practice shot, pick a specific aiming point — a post, a flag, a marker. Go through your setup routine. Then hit. This is not slowing you down; it is making the reps meaningful.

Short game deserves half your time

Most golfers spend 80% of their practice on full swings and 20% on the short game. The actual distribution of strokes in a round is roughly the opposite — especially for higher handicaps. Putting, chipping, and pitching from 50 to 100 yards account for a disproportionate number of the shots that determine your score.

If you only have 45 minutes, consider spending 20 on full swing and 25 on short game. Putting and chipping can be done at home in the evenings. Full swing usually cannot. Use the range for what only the range can give you.

How to evaluate the session after

At the end, ask yourself: did I get better at the one thing I was working on? Not: did I hit it well? You can have a technically improving session that looks messy and a technically stalled session that produces some good shots. The good shots during block practice are often not the ones to trust yet — they might be the result of a compensation, not the actual fix.

Keep simple notes. One sentence. "Kept the trail elbow closer in — hitting more solid contact but still pulling left occasionally." That sentence is worth more than trying to remember how it went.
