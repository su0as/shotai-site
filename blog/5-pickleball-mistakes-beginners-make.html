5 Pickleball Mistakes Beginners Make (And How to Fix Them)

Pickleball looks simple from the sideline, which is exactly why beginners often build bad habits without realizing it. The court is small, the scoring is fast, and when rallies go wrong it is not always obvious why. These five mistakes account for the majority of technique problems in players who are new to the game — and all of them are fixable with a small amount of deliberate practice.

Mistake 1: Swinging too hard

This is the most common and most damaging mistake in the beginner game. Players bring a power mindset from tennis, baseball, or just general athletic instinct — they swing hard because hard feels like trying. In pickleball, it usually works against you.

The kitchen is 7 feet from the net. When you swing hard from that distance, the ball either hits the net or sails long. Hard swings from the baseline are easier to control, but they still create more pace than most beginners can direct accurately. The game rewards placement over power at almost every level below competitive play.

The fix: Drop your swing speed by 40% and focus on where you want the ball to land rather than how hard you can hit it. You will make more errors by swinging softer before you start making contact errors from lack of pace. If you use video review, watch your backswing — if it is going past your hip, that is your cue to shorten up.

Mistake 2: Gripping with the wrong part of the handle

Many new players hold the paddle too far toward the end of the handle — similar to how they might hold a tennis racket for maximum leverage. In pickleball, this reduces touch and control. The paddle face is smaller, the swings are shorter, and precision matters more than leverage.

A related grip mistake is squeezing too tightly. Tight grips create tension in the forearm and wrist that kills soft touch — the one skill you need most in this game. Players who are gripping too hard tend to pop the ball up on dinks instead of keeping it low.

The fix: Choke up slightly on the grip and hold the paddle at a tension level of about four or five out of ten — firm enough to stay in control, light enough to feel the ball on contact. Check this every few rallies, especially if your dinks are floating higher than you intend.

Mistake 3: Not moving into the kitchen quickly enough

The kitchen line is where you want to be for most of the rally. Players who hang back near the baseline or mid-court are stuck hitting balls from a lower position, giving their opponents more options and more time. The general principle in pickleball is: serve, return deep, and move forward as quickly as the third shot allows.

Beginners often hesitate because moving forward feels risky — what if the opponent hits the ball past them? That thinking leads to a passive game where you are always reacting instead of dictating. The answer is not to charge the net recklessly, but to move forward with purpose after each shot and reset your ready position at the kitchen.

The fix: After you return serve, take two steps forward and stop in your ready position. After each shot in the transition zone, keep advancing until you reach the kitchen. You will get passed occasionally. Accept it as the cost of learning correct positioning.

Mistake 4: Opening the paddle face too early on dinks

This mistake is almost invisible until you see it on video. When beginners dink, they often tilt the paddle face skyward during the swing — sometimes before contact, sometimes right at the moment of hitting. This puts the ball high over the net, which gives the opponent an easy ball to attack.

The paddle face on a dink should be roughly vertical at contact, not open. The arc of the shot comes from swing direction, not face angle. Players who open the face are compensating for a swing that is coming too flat — so the ball would go into the net without the face adjustment.

The fix: Keep the paddle face vertical and swing slightly upward from below the ball. Contact should happen in front of your body. If you watch your dink on video and the paddle is opening before you reach the ball, focus on keeping the bottom edge of the paddle lower than the top throughout the swing.

Mistake 5: No consistent follow-through

Beginners often stop the paddle at the point of contact — a jab or a poke rather than a stroke. This produces inconsistent direction because the paddle face angle at contact changes shot to shot. A proper follow-through extends toward your target and holds that line for a moment after the ball leaves the paddle.

This matters most on groundstrokes from the baseline, but it affects dinks and volleys too. Players who stab at the ball get wildly varying results from similar swings. Players who follow through develop a consistent strike pattern much faster.

The fix: After each shot, hold your follow-through for one full second. It will feel deliberate and slow at first. That is fine. You are building a reference point. In video review, look at where your paddle is pointing one second after contact — it should be aimed at the intended target, not dropped toward the ground or crossed over your body.

Why these mistakes stick so long

All five of these errors share one thing: they feel correct when you are making them. Swinging hard feels powerful. A tightly gripped paddle feels secure. Stopping the swing at contact feels controlled. The problem is that pickleball gives you instant but incomplete feedback. You know when you missed. You do not always know why.

Video review breaks that cycle. When you can watch your own swing, you can see whether the mistake you are trying to fix is actually changing between sessions — or whether it is still there despite feeling different.
