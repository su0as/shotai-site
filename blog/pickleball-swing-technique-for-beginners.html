Pickleball Swing Technique for Beginners

Most new pickleball players come from tennis or a racket sport background and make the same mistake: they swing like they are on a bigger court. The fundamentals of good pickleball technique are compact, controlled, and very different from what feels natural on day one. Here is what actually matters when you are starting out.

Why pickleball technique is different from tennis

The court is roughly a third the size of a tennis court, which changes everything. A long backswing that works fine for tennis adds energy you cannot control at close range. The game rewards compact strokes, soft hands, and patience near the kitchen line — not power. If you are coming from tennis, your instincts are going to fight you for the first few weeks.

The good news is that pickleball rewards improvement quickly. Because the swings are shorter and the mechanics are simpler, a beginner who practices with purpose can build a solid baseline technique in a few months, not years.

Grip: the continental is your foundation

Most coaches recommend starting with a continental grip — imagine shaking hands with the paddle handle, with the V formed by your thumb and index finger sitting on the top bevel. This grip feels awkward at first but gives you the most flexibility. It works for forehands, backhands, volleys, and dinks without having to rotate the paddle between shots.

Avoid a full western or eastern grip when you are starting out. These can produce topspin forehands, but they restrict your backhand and make net play much harder. Build the continental first, then experiment as your game develops.

Grip pressure matters as much as grip position. Hold the paddle firmly enough that it does not twist on contact, but loose enough that your wrist stays relaxed. Squeezing too hard kills touch and leads to arm fatigue.

Ready position and footwork basics

The ready position is the one thing most beginners skip entirely. Before every shot, you should be in an athletic stance: feet shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent, weight on the balls of your feet, paddle held in front of your body at roughly waist height. This is not a natural resting posture, so you have to train it deliberately.

From the ready position, move to the ball with small split steps rather than big running strides. At the kitchen line especially, most shots are made with minimal footwork — the adjustment is a small step or a weight shift, not a lunge. Players who lunge constantly are compensating for late preparation, which comes back to the ready position.

One drill: between every shot in a rally, reset your feet and check that your paddle is up and forward. It feels slow at first but it trains the habit that keeps you from getting caught flat-footed.

Forehand mechanics: short backswing, clean contact

A good pickleball forehand does not look like a tennis forehand. The backswing should be short — the paddle barely goes past your hip. From there, drive through the ball with your body rotating toward the target, not just your arm swinging. Contact happens in front of your body, ideally at waist height or slightly below.

The follow-through finishes low to high for drives, or stops flat for dinks and drops. Beginners often wave the paddle through the ball instead of committing to a direction. Focus on where you want the paddle to point at the moment of contact and let the follow-through happen naturally from there.

Common mistakes on the forehand: opening the face too early (causes pop-ups), contacting the ball too close to your body (reduces control), and finishing too high on soft shots (gives the ball too much height over the net).

Backhand: the shot that often decides matches

Many beginners avoid the backhand instinctively, running around it to hit a weaker forehand instead. This is the wrong habit to build. A continental grip backhand is one of the most reliable shots in pickleball, and learning it early pays dividends for years.

For a two-handed backhand, keep the non-dominant hand active — it guides the paddle face and adds stability. For a one-handed backhand, focus on leading with the elbow slightly and making contact in front of your hip rather than beside it. The backswing on a backhand should be even shorter than the forehand. It is almost a push rather than a swing at the kitchen line.

The most common backhand mistake is letting the elbow drop before contact. This opens the paddle face and causes the ball to float. Keep the elbow roughly level with the paddle during the swing path and the face angle becomes much easier to control.

The dink: the shot that separates beginners from intermediate players

The dink is a soft, arcing shot that lands in the kitchen (the non-volley zone). It is not a defensive shot — it is a strategic one. Good dinking forces your opponent to hit up on the ball, which gives you a better opportunity to attack. Players who cannot dink consistently will be pushed back from the kitchen and put into defensive positions constantly.

Technically, a good dink starts with an open paddle face, a short compact swing, and a wrist that stays locked through contact. You are not trying to flick or add spin — you are placing the ball just over the net tape and having it drop quickly into the kitchen. The target should be roughly three to six inches over the net, not higher.

Practice dinking before you practice anything else. It is the one shot where technique problems show up most clearly, and it is the one most directly tied to rally length and point construction at all levels of play.

How to use video to check your own form

The biggest challenge with self-coaching is that what you feel rarely matches what you do. You might feel like you are keeping a short backswing when your video shows a full tennis backswing. You might feel like your paddle face is square when it is actually closing at contact. Video removes the guesswork.

Set your phone up on a tripod at roughly waist height, angled to capture your full swing. Record a block of dinks, then groundstrokes, then volleys separately so you can review each in isolation. Look for: backswing length, contact point relative to your body, and where your paddle face is pointing at contact. These three checkpoints cover most beginner problems.

The value of this approach is that you can do it alone, between sessions, without needing a coach present. One review per week comparing your current video to your last one is enough to track whether your corrections are actually sticking.
